HOMEPAGE

Engineering the Family

China spent almost four decades limiting births and now is spending money to encourage them. This site looks at the doctors, midwives, and all the families that were caught between the policies of the Chinese Government.

This project will look at China's family planning policies all around the nation, starting with the later longer fewer policy of 1973, through the one-child policy of 1979–80 to the two-child policy of 2016 and the three-child policy of 2021. The angle that I will be focusing on is the enforcement of the policies: how the fact that the same national policy was enforced differently depending on whether you live in an urban or rural area, and the methods used, like IUDs, sterilization, and abortions, were the mechanisms the state used to get into people's lives.

What makes this sociologically interesting is that the policy was marketed as protecting mothers and children. Whether that was true depended heavily on where you lived and your social class. Many posters talked about healthier mothers and healthier babies. Barefoot doctors and local cadres did the work of making the quotas happen. A wealthier urban family could pay a fine and move on; however, a rural family would not have the same wealth and would have to go through hard work and labor, while some ethnic minorities were allowed more children. The final result was one of the most skewed male-to-female ratios. And now the government is trying to fix it forty years later by trying to talk people back into having children.

The question that kept coming back was: how does the same authority justify restricting births from happening and then decades later start begging for more of them? The answer I came up with based on the sources was that the number that the government wanted changed, but the idea that the government gets to control family size did not change.

Historical context

1973–2021: from post-Mao population anxiety through the era's quota system to the reversal in demographics.

Geographic context

One policy, many governments: many urban one-child households, a 1.5-child rule for rural communities, and some ethnic minorities allowed more children.

Sociological question

What happens to trust in medicine when the healthcare system is enforcing population policies?
Timeline · 1973–2021

Voluntary to Forceful

Each moment describes a change in the process and each moment brings a huge change in enforcement and policy.

1973

“Later, longer, fewer” becomes national policy

By 1973, China's family planning campaign is pushing for later marriages, bigger gaps between births, and fewer kids per couple. Fertility starts dropping many years before the one-child rule even exists.

Source: chineseposters.net, Population Policy
Source: Junsen Zhang, “The Evolution of China's One-Child Policy and Its Effects on Family Outcomes,” Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2017
1975

Birth control is shown as health, not control

A government poster says that “practicing birth control is beneficial for the protection of the health of mother and child.” At this point, the message is still persuasion, but the persuasion gives way to quotas a few years later.

Source: 1975 poster, chineseposters.net
1979–80

The one-child policy gets adopted

Families that go over the birth quota of one get penalized. Local officials are judged on enforcement, which gives them strong incentives to press families to comply, and a one-child certificate is issued to couples who stop at one. Through the 1980s, the healthcare system provided IUDs and sterilization.

Source: Hardee-Cleaveland & Banister (1988), as quoted in Zhang, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2017
Source: chineseposters.net, Population Policy
2016

The two-child policy becomes official

After about 36 years, every couple is now allowed to have up to two children. Births go up for a bit and then start falling again. The old rural 1.5-child rule is gone, but its effect on the male-to-female ratio still shows up in the data.

Source (birth numbers): CGTN, “Is China seeing a baby boom five years into two-child policy?”
Source (1.5-child rule and sex ratio): Jiang & Zhang, “Recent sex ratio at birth in China,” BMJ Global Health, 2021
2021

Three-child policy

A few weeks after census data showed the population is aging fast, the government raises the limit to three children in May 2021. Births had already dropped to 12 million in 2020, the fewest since the 1960s. It is much easier for the government to stop people from having kids than to get them to have more.

Source: Al Jazeera, “China allows families to have 3 children in major policy shift”
Source: Al Jazeera The Stream, “China's three-child policy: Too little too late?”
Event analysis · 1979–80

Quota at the Doorstep

Among the five events in the timeline, the adoption of the one-child policy is the event that defined the power of the Chinese government within the household. Before this, the campaigns relied more on persuasion.

The significant thing about 1979-80 is not that the Chinese government had just started limiting births. The government had already started doing that from the early 1970s through many campaigns. What changed, however, was who was able to control the decision. Before the one-child policy, couples were mostly encouraged to have fewer children, though quota attempts had already begun in the 1970s. After the policy began, the government decided for the families how many children each village was allowed to have.

The system worked through the government hierarchy, not just the healthcare system. Birth limits were passed down from the higher government to the communities all throughout China. Eventually, people were given jobs specifically to make sure the area stayed within its birth limit.

“Birth quotas were passed downwards through the administrative hierarchy until each small group received its allocated number of births.”

— chineseposters.net, Population Policy

This system also helps explain why the enforcement was harsh. Local officials were judged on whether they met their targets or not, so they made sure families followed these rules. Junsen Zhang, in the Journal of Economic Perspectives, explains how the pressures affected the enforcement of the policies.

“Provincial and prefectural officials had strong incentives to require or even force local residents to comply with the one-child policy.”

— Hardee-Cleaveland and Banister (1988), as cited in Junsen Zhang, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2017
Why it matters

The implication of this from a social perspective is that the family planning clinic became an extension of the state. Local health clinics gave people basic care and birth control, but they could also report pregnancies to the government as well. This likely led families not to trust the healthcare system even after the end of the one-child policy.
01 · Synthesis

Health as Language

The family planning campaign that was pushed can be explained by the intricate mechanics of the language in the poster. These four posters below show that health was not the only reason for the policy. But it was used to make the policy seem like a good thing for people. And for fifteen years, the reasons for having fewer children changed, but the government’s goal stayed the same.

Multimedia · four posters, 1972–1987

When looking at the posters in order, they each give different reasons, all pointing towards the same goal. In 1972, people were told to have fewer children for the revolution; then, in 1974, birth control was brought to people’s doors. In 1986 it was a national policy, and by 1987 it was connected to having healthier children. The reasons changed, but the government’s goal stayed the same.

The first two posters are also very important, as China already had a strong family planning program before the one-child policy began. Economist Junsen Zhang explains that fertility had fallen during the 1970s, meaning that the suggestions given had a major effect even before the one-child policy.

The message in poster 2 was connected to real life, as Birth control was provided through clinics and doctors, and families depended on them for medical needs.

“The delivery of contraceptives was closely tied in with the provision of basic health care by local clinics in urban areas and by the barefoot doctors in the countryside.”

— chineseposters.net, Population Policy

This is very important because family planning and health care services in China became connected. If birth control had been controlled by someone else besides the government, people would’ve separated it. Instead, the same workers who provided the care would also be involved in enforcing the policies. Making the family cautious about trusting the government with their health.

Poster 4 shows how the change occurred in how the policy was explained. By the mid-1980s, the government used science to support family planning. The focus became not how many children people had but the so-called quality of the population.

“By the mid-1980s, the urgings to limit the number of children increasingly were grounded in authoritative scientific justifications that stressed that in this way, the gene pool could be improved.”

— chineseposters.net, Population Policy
Why it matters

This matters because health was one of the main ways that the government carried out the family planning policies. Clinics and health workers were not used to just providing medical care, but they also helped enforce birth limits. Because of this, families started to connect health care to government control, and it would affect people’s choices in trusting the healthcare system and using it.
02 · Synthesis

Two Chinas

The one-child policy is often described as just one rule for all of China, but often it was not the case. Where a person lived affected how strict the rule was, mainly in how families were watched and the consequences if they broke the rule.

The name one-child policy makes it seem like the policy was more equal than it was. In cities, the rule was strictly one child, and it was much easier to enforce because of committees keeping track of the number of children families were having. In rural areas, many provinces used a less strict rule called the 1.5 child policy. This allowed families to have a second child if their first child was a girl. Minority groups, especially ethnic ones composing about nine percent of the population, were allowed to have more than one children.

Urban

Usually one child very close monitoring. Work units and committees make it easier. In some cases, having a second child could put government jobs at risk, because of this people chose to follow the rule.

Rural

Many rural areas followed the 1.5 child rule meaning that the family could have a second child if their first was a girl. Families were not as monitored closely and were willing to pay fines for having another child. Children were also important because many rural families needed them for labor and support as parents age.

Ethnic minorities

Ethnic Minority families, about nine percent of the whole population were allowed to have more children than non ethnic chinese families most of the time. Showing that the government was willing to change the limit for difference groups

The propaganda posters also show who the audience of the campaigns were for. Many posters showed apartments, clean clinics and hospitals. The website chineseposters.net noticed the same pattern

“What really leaps out from these posters is their urban bias, and this should give food for thought about the effectiveness of the ‘One Child’-campaign as a whole.”

— chineseposters.net, Population Policy

This is very important because urban families were already easier to control and they were watched more closely, and usually lived in smaller closer homes, they could also face many consequences at work if they had another child. Rural families were also harder to control, but they were also shown less in the propaganda.

Rural families also had reasons for wanting more children. Many did not have pensions so the children were expected to support their parents when they became older. Some families took the fine for an extra child as something that was worth paying because the child could help the family later. This meant that the penalty of not following the policy was completely different and depended heavily on where you lived.

The government eventually realized that the rule was very hard to enforce in rural areas. Yi Zeng and Therese Hesketh explain this very thing in The Lancet.

“In rural areas, this rule was particularly unpopular and deemed virtually unenforceable.”

— Yi Zeng and Therese Hesketh, “The effects of China's universal two-child policy,” The Lancet, 2016

Because the rule was so hard to enforce, the government changed it in many rural areas. Starting in 1984, couples in most rural areas could have a second child if their first one was a girl. It became known as the 1.5 child policy.

These exceptions were not loopholes, but they were changes made by the government because the same rule was not enforced everywhere.

Why it matters

This matters because the one-child policy was not experienced the same way all across China. Urban families faced stricter rules and were more closely monitored, while rural families and minorities had more exceptions. Meaning that where a person lived and their social class affected how the policy had an impact on them. These differences also shaped effects later, including the boys-to-girls birth ratio.
03 · Synthesis

Missing Daughters

Roughly 105 boys are born for every 100 girls, everywhere in the world, without anyone arranging it. China spent two decades well outside that range. The pattern in the numbers is not the one most people expect, and it is the clearest evidence that enforcement worked differently from province to province.

The sex ratio at birth is the number of boys born for every 100 girls. Quanbao Jiang and Cuiling Zhang, writing in BMJ Global Health, treat anything from about 102 to 107 as the normal biological range. A number above that does not happen naturally, so it has to be explained by something people are doing, whether that is sex-selective abortion after an ultrasound, or daughters going unregistered.

The obvious guess would be that the strictest rule produced the worst imbalance, since a family allowed only one child has only one chance at a son. The census data says otherwise.

“According to China's 2000 census, the SRB in one-child policy areas was 111.6, lower than the SRB of 124.7 in 1.5-child policy areas but higher than that in two-child policy areas.”

— Jiang and Zhang, “Recent sex ratio at birth in China,” BMJ Global Health, 2021

The looser rule produced the worse imbalance, by a wide margin. The reason is in how the 1.5-child rule was written. A rural couple whose first child was a boy was finished. A couple whose first child was a girl got exactly one more birth, and that second birth was the last chance they would ever have for a son. The rule did not just permit a second child, it identified in advance which families were under pressure and told them precisely when the pressure applied. A policy meant as a concession to rural families ended up aiming the decision at daughters.

Because the rules were assigned province by province, the effect is mappable. The map below shades each province by its sex ratio at birth across four censuses, and the darkest provinces track the 1.5-child provinces rather than the strict one-child cities.

Reading the four maps in order also shows the imbalance easing. Zeng and Hesketh, in The Lancet, put the national peak at 121 boys per 100 girls in 2005, falling to 116 by 2014, but with ratios reaching as high as 140 in parts of rural central China. A national average of 116 is already far outside the biological range, and a local figure of 140 describes places where a large share of girls who should have been born were not. The decline since has been helped by the rules being relaxed and by the ban on ultrasound sex determination for non-medical reasons. What has not happened is a full return to normal in the provinces that ran the 1.5-child rule. The chart below groups provinces by which rule they used, and the gap between those groups outlives the rules themselves.

The three lines never converge. Jiang and Zhang follow them past every loosening of the policy and find the old grouping still holding:

“Even after the one-child policy was replaced by a selective two-child policy in 2013 and a universal two-child policy in 2016, the provinces with originally 1.5-child policy still had the highest SRB, especially Fujian, Anhui, Jiangxi, Hunan and Hubei provinces.”

— Jiang and Zhang, BMJ Global Health, 2021

The rule that created the gap was gone, and the gap stayed. That is the part worth noticing. A birth limit can be repealed by announcement, but the preference for sons that the limit sharpened was already there before the policy and did not end with it. The policy did not invent the preference, it gave it a deadline.

It also matters how this was carried out. Sex selection ran through the same medical system as everything else in this story, through the ultrasound and the clinic, which is why China eventually had to ban non-medical sex determination outright. The UNFPA argues in its 2024 policy brief on the sex ratio that the imbalance should be understood as a symptom of entrenched social and economic injustice against girls and women, not as a demographic accident to be corrected with numbers.

Why it matters

The sex ratio is the place where the two Chinas of the previous page stop being an argument and become a measurable fact. It also shows the limits of governing a family by quota. The state could set how many children a household was allowed, but it could not set which children people wanted, and the space between those two things was filled by the missing daughters this page is named after.
