HOMEPAGE

Engineering the Family

China spent almost four decades limiting births and now is spending money to encourage them. This site looks at the doctors, midwives, and all the families that were caught between the policies of the Chinese Government.

This project will look at China's family planning policies all around the nation, starting with the later longer fewer policy of 1973, through the one-child policy of 1979–80 to the two-child policy of 2016 and the three-child policy of 2021. The angle that I will be focusing on is the enforcement of the policies: how the fact that the same national policy was enforced differently depending on whether you live in an urban or rural area, and the methods used, like IUDs, sterilization, and abortions, were the mechanisms the state used to get into people's lives.

What makes this sociologically interesting is that the policy was marketed as protecting mothers and children. Whether that was true depended heavily on where you lived and your social class. Many posters talked about healthier mothers and healthier babies. Midwives and local cadres did the work of making the quotas happen. A wealthier urban family could pay a fine and move on; however, a rural family would not have the same wealth and would have to go through hard work and labor, while some ethnic minorities were exempted from the law. The final result was one of the most skewed male-to-female ratios. And now the government is trying to fix it forty years later by trying to talk people back into having children.

The question that kept coming back was: how does the same authority justify restricting births from happening and then decades later start begging for more of them? The answer I came up with based on the sources was that the number that the government wanted changed, but the idea that the government gets to control family size did not change.

Historical context

1973–2021: from post-Mao population anxiety through the era's quota system to the reversal in demographics.

Geographic context

One policy, many governments: many urban one-child households, a 1.5-child rule for rural communities, and some ethnic minorities exempted.

Sociological question

What happens to trust in medicine when the healthcare system is enforcing population policies?
Timeline · 1973–2021

Voluntary to Forceful

Each moment describes a change in the process and each moment brings a huge change in enforcement and policy.

1973

“Later, longer, fewer” becomes national policy

By 1973, China's family planning campaign is pushing for later marriages, bigger gaps between births, and fewer kids per couple. Fertility starts dropping many years before the one-child rule even exists.

Source: chineseposters.net, Population Policy
Source: Junsen Zhang, “The Evolution of China's One-Child Policy and Its Effects on Family Outcomes,” Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2017
1975

Birth control is shown as health, not control

A government poster says that “practicing birth control is beneficial for the protection of the health of mother and child.” At this point, the message is still persuasion, but the persuasion gives way to quotas a few years later.

Source: 1975 poster, chineseposters.net
1979–80

The one-child policy gets adopted

Families that go over the birth quota of one get penalized. Local officials are judged on enforcement, which gives them strong incentives to press families to comply, and a one-child certificate is issued to couples who stop at one. Through the 1980s, the healthcare system provided IUDs and sterilization.

Source: Hardee-Cleaveland & Banister (1988), as quoted in Zhang, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2017
Source: chineseposters.net, Population Policy
2016

The two-child policy becomes official

After about 40 years, every couple is now allowed to have up to two children. Births go up for a bit and then start falling again. The old rural 1.5-child rule is gone, but its effect on the male-to-female ratio still shows up in the data.

Source (birth numbers): CGTN, “Is China seeing a baby boom five years into two-child policy?”
Source (1.5-child rule and sex ratio): Jiang & Zhang, “Recent sex ratio at birth in China,” BMJ Global Health, 2021
2021

Three-child policy

A few weeks after census data showed the population is aging fast, the government raises the limit to three children in May 2021. Births had already dropped to 12 million in 2020, the fewest since the 1960s. It is much easier for the government to stop people from having kids than to get them to have more.

Source: Al Jazeera, “China allows families to have 3 children in major policy shift”
Source: Al Jazeera The Stream, “China's three-child policy: Too little too late?”
Event analysis · 1979–80

The quota reaches the doorstep

Of the five events on the timeline, the adoption of the one-child policy is the one that changed what the state was allowed to do inside a household. Everything before it was a campaign. After it, a birth was an allocation.

The important thing about 1979–80 is not that China started limiting births. It had been doing that since the early 1970s. What changed is who made the decision. Before the one-child policy, the campaign asked couples to want fewer children. After it, the number of children a village was permitted to have was set above them and handed down.

The mechanism was administrative, not medical. Quotas moved down through the bureaucracy until they reached the smallest unit of social life, and at that level a family planning worker was responsible for meeting the number.

“Birth quotas were passed downwards through the administrative hierarchy until each small group received its allocated number of births.”

— chineseposters.net, Population Policy

That structure explains why enforcement turned coercive without anyone at the top ordering coercion. Local officials were assessed on whether their area met its target, which made a birth somebody's job performance. Junsen Zhang, writing in the Journal of Economic Perspectives, describes what that incentive produced, citing Hardee-Cleaveland and Banister's 1988 study of implementation.

“Provincial and prefectural officials had strong incentives to require or even force local residents to comply with the one-child policy.”

— Hardee-Cleaveland and Banister (1988), as cited in Junsen Zhang, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2017
Why it matters

The societal implication is that the clinic became an arm of the state. The same local health network that delivered contraception and basic care was the network that reported on births, which meant the person checking on a pregnancy and the person enforcing the quota could be the same person. That is the part that outlasted the policy: a health system that many families had reason to approach carefully.
01 · Synthesis

Health as language

Population control was almost never sold as population control. It was sold as health, then as revolution, then as law, then as genetic quality — and the vocabulary shifted whenever the state needed a new reason.

The reason medicine is the right lens for this topic is that the campaign travelled through the health system. Contraception was not distributed by police; it arrived with basic care, carried by the same people who handled everything else medical in a village.

“The delivery of contraceptives was closely tied in with the provision of basic health care by local clinics in urban areas and by the barefoot doctors in the countryside.”

— chineseposters.net, Population Policy

Because the message travelled with care, refusing the message meant refusing your caregiver. The posters below show how the justification kept changing while the demand stayed the same.

Multimedia · four posters, 1972–1987

A sequence from the collection at chineseposters.net, read in order as the argument the state made to its own citizens.

Takeaway

Across fifteen years the reason changes four times — revolution, health, law, genetics — and the instruction never changes. That is what makes the health framing worth taking seriously as rhetoric rather than as description: it was one available justification among several, and the state reached for whichever one was working.
02 · Synthesis

Two Chinas, one policy

The one-child policy was never one policy. What it meant for a family depended on whether they lived in a city, in a village, or in a region with an ethnic-minority exemption.

The rule that most rural families actually lived under was not one child. In most provinces a rural couple whose first child was a girl could have a second — the arrangement usually called the 1.5-child policy. Zeng and Hesketh, writing in The Lancet, describe how badly the stricter rule travelled outside the cities.

“In rural areas, this rule was particularly unpopular and deemed virtually unenforceable.”

— Yi Zeng and Therese Hesketh, The Lancet, 2016

The gap was also a class gap. A fine is a deterrent only if it hurts, and in the countryside the arithmetic often ran the other way: an extra child was a worker and an old-age pension in a place with neither. The poster collection itself records how that calculation looked from the village.

“Many peasants considered the fines that had to be paid when more children were born as a small investment that the children would repay manifold in the future.”

— chineseposters.net, Population Policy
Urban

Dense oversight through neighbourhood committees, salaried jobs the state could dock, and housing the state allocated. Compliance was high because non-compliance was visible.

Rural

Thinner monitoring, the 1.5-child rule in most provinces, and an economic logic that made another child an asset rather than a cost.

Exempted

Ethnic minorities, roughly nine percent of the population, faced relaxed limits — a reminder that the state could choose where the rule applied.

Takeaway

The propaganda itself gives the divide away. Writing about the same collection of posters, chineseposters.net notes that “what really leaps out from these posters is their urban bias,” and calls the absence of posters addressing the policy in rural terms “simply astonishing.” The campaign pictured a city family because the city was where it worked.
03 · Synthesis

The missing daughters

The clearest evidence that enforcement varied by place is written into the sex ratio. Where the rule bent, it bent in a way that put pressure on daughters.

Roughly 105 boys are born for every 100 girls without intervention. The 1.5-child rule created a trap that the one-child rule did not: a rural couple whose first child was a girl got one more birth, and that birth had to be a son. The result is visible in the data — and it is the areas with the looser rule that show the worst imbalance.

“According to China's 2000 census, the SRB in one-child policy areas was 111.6, lower than the SRB of 124.7 in 1.5-child policy areas.”

— Quanbao Jiang and Cuiling Zhang, BMJ Global Health, 2021

The pattern did not disappear when the policy did. Years after every couple was allowed two children, the same provinces were still identifiable in the numbers.

“The provinces with originally 1.5-child policy still had the highest SRB, especially Fujian, Anhui, Jiangxi, Hunan and Hubei provinces.”

— Jiang and Zhang, BMJ Global Health, 2021
Takeaway

The UNFPA argues that sex selection is “a symptom of pervasive social, cultural, political and economic injustices against girls and women,” which is the right correction to make here. The policy did not invent son preference. What it did was take an existing preference and attach a hard limit to it, so that a family acting on that preference had exactly one chance to do it.
04 · Synthesis

The backfire

China now wants more births and cannot get them. The state that once set the number has discovered that permission is not the same as persuasion.

The long view complicates the story the policy tells about itself. Fertility was already falling steeply through the 1970s, and by the time the one-child rule arrived the steepest part of the decline had happened. It has kept falling since the rule was lifted, which suggests the policy was riding a trend at least as much as causing one.

The relaxations have not moved the line. Two children in 2016 produced a brief rise and then three consecutive years of decline; three children in 2021 arrived after births had already fallen to 12 million. The obstacle is no longer the law, and the sociologist Wang Feng makes that point bluntly.

“It's outdated. What China needs is not another state policy, but rather a better and fairer society.”

— Wang Feng, quoted in The Guardian, 31 May 2021

What replaced the quota is cost. Reporting by Al Jazeera on the economics of the three-child policy found families pointing at housing, education and childcare rather than at permission — the state can lift a limit, but it cannot lift the price of raising a child.

2016

Two children permitted. Births rise to 17.86 million, then fall for three straight years.

2020

12 million births, the fewest since the 1960s, recorded by the census that prompted the next reversal.

2021

Three children permitted. Commentators call it too little, too late.

Takeaway

The through-line across all four decades is the assumption underneath both policies. In 1980 the state decided the correct number of children was one; in 2021 it decided the correct number was three. What never changed is the premise that the number is the government's to set — and the reversal shows how much easier that premise is to act on in one direction than the other.
